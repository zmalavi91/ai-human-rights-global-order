# Research Methods for AI, Technology, and Human Rights

## Purpose

This document presents a research framework for examining artificial intelligence, digital surveillance, technology governance, and their effects on human rights and international politics.

The framework combines Political Science, International Relations, human-rights analysis, international law, public policy, ethics, and institutional research.

---

## 1. Research Problem Formulation

A research project should begin by identifying:

- The technology or digital system under study.
- The institution, government, company, or actor involved.
- The affected population.
- The relevant political, legal, ethical, or human-rights problem.
- The geographical and historical context.
- The research gap.
- The practical significance of the research.

### Example

**Broad topic:** Artificial intelligence and human rights.

**Focused problem:** The effect of automated decision-making on equal access to public welfare.

**Research question:** How does the use of automated eligibility systems affect transparency, non-discrimination, and access to remedy?

---

## 2. Research Question Development

Strong research questions should be:

- Clear.
- Specific.
- Researchable.
- Conceptually meaningful.
- Connected to evidence.
- Limited in scope.
- Relevant to a wider academic or policy debate.

### Types of Research Questions

#### Descriptive

What does the system do?

#### Explanatory

Why was the system created or deployed?

#### Comparative

How does the system differ across countries or institutions?

#### Normative

Is the system ethically or legally justified?

#### Evaluative

How effective is the system?

#### Policy-oriented

What should governments, companies, or institutions do?

### Question-Building Formula

```text
How does [technology or policy]
affect [rights, institution, or community]
under [specific political or legal conditions]
in [place and period]?
```

## 4. Conceptual Framework

A conceptual framework should define the principal concepts used in the research.

Possible concepts include:

- Artificial intelligence.
- Digital surveillance.
- Privacy.
- Freedom of expression.
- Human dignity.
- Equality.
- Algorithmic discrimination.
- Transparency.
- Explainability.
- Accountability.
- Human oversight.
- Democratic governance.
- International order.
- Strategic autonomy.
- Human security.

Each concept should include:

1. Definition.
2. Main dimensions.
3. Relationship to other concepts.
4. Indicators or observable features.
5. Relevance to the research question.

## 6. Case-Study Method

A case study should clearly define:

- The case.
- The reason for selecting it.
- The period under investigation.
- The relevant actors.
- The political and legal context.
- The evidence base.
- The analytical framework.
- The limits of generalization.

### Case-Selection Criteria

A case may be selected because it is:

- Typical.
- Critical.
- Extreme.
- Influential.
- Controversial.
- Under-researched.
- Comparative.
- Relevant to a major policy debate.

## 8. Qualitative Document Analysis

Documents may include:

- Laws.
- Regulations.
- Court judgments.
- Government policies.
- Parliamentary debates.
- Official reports.
- Corporate policies.
- International agreements.
- UN documents.
- Human-rights reports.
- Academic publications.
- Civil-society reports.
- Public statements.

### Document-Analysis Questions

1. Who produced the document?
2. What is its purpose?
3. Which concepts are emphasized?
4. Which actors are represented?
5. Which actors are absent?
6. What rights are recognized?
7. What risks are ignored?
8. What assumptions are made?
9. What institutional responsibilities are identified?
10. What remedies are available?

## 9. Critical Discourse Analysis

Critical discourse analysis can examine how language constructs:

- Security threats.
- Dangerous populations.
- National interests.
- Technological progress.
- Digital enemies.
- Privacy.
- Public safety.
- Terrorism.
- Innovation.
- Humanitarian protection.

### Discourse Questions

- How is the technology described?
- What problem is it said to solve?
- Who is portrayed as a risk?
- Which rights are treated as secondary?
- What forms of authority are normalized?
- Which voices are excluded?
- What political interests are advanced?

## 13. Evidence Evaluation

Evidence should be assessed according to:

- Authority.
- Reliability.
- Relevance.
- Timeliness.
- Transparency.
- Methodological quality.
- Possible bias.
- Corroboration.
- Limitations.

Use a hierarchy of evidence appropriate to the research question:

1. Primary legal and policy documents.
2. Official institutional records.
3. Court judgments.
4. International-organization reports.
5. Peer-reviewed academic research.
6. Independent research reports.
7. Reputable journalism.
8. Expert commentary.
9. Social-media content, used cautiously and verified.

---

## 14. Research Ethics

Researchers should:

- Protect personal data.
- Avoid exposing vulnerable individuals.
- Anonymize sensitive information.
- Obtain consent where human participants are involved.
- Avoid collecting unnecessary data.
- Protect research participants from retaliation.
- Disclose methodological limitations.
- Represent evidence accurately.
- Avoid fabricated or unverifiable claims.
- Distinguish analysis from advocacy.
- Respect copyright.
- Secure research files.

## 16. Quality-Control Checklist

Before publishing research, ask:

- Is the research question clear?
- Are the concepts defined?
- Is the methodology appropriate?
- Are the sources reliable?
- Are claims supported by evidence?
- Are alternative explanations considered?
- Are limitations acknowledged?
- Are affected communities represented fairly?
- Are legal claims verified?
- Is confidential information protected?
- Are recommendations practical?
- Are citations complete?
- Is the distinction between fact and opinion clear?
